# Pigeon Poxvirus (PiPV) in Racing Pigeons

Pigeon Poxvirus is a common viral pathogen of racing pigeons. This page describes its transmission routes, signs, diagnosis and control points.

## Plain-language take (an analogy)

Pigeon pox is like **"wart-like pimples growing on the skin and in the oral cavity"** — mosquito bites or breaks in the skin give the virus its opening; the pox lesions look alarming, but most cases are self-limiting, and secondary infection is what you need to guard against.

## Overview

Pigeon Poxvirus belongs to the Poxviridae and is a common viral pathogen of pigeons, causing cutaneous and mucosal lesions. Transmission is closely tied to mosquito activity, with a peak in summer and autumn.

## Pathogen characteristics

Poxviruses are large DNA viruses with strong resistance to drying; they can survive for long periods in shed scabs and contaminated environments.

## Transmission

Spread mainly mechanically by mosquitoes and other blood-feeding insects, and also by direct contact through skin breaks and pecking injuries; virus shed with fallen scabs contaminates the environment and forms a persistent source of infection.

## Common signs

**Note**: the signs below **cannot be used on their own to confirm a diagnosis**. Several diseases can produce similar signs, and laboratory testing is the basis for confirmation.

- Pox lesions and scabs on unfeathered skin (eyelids, corners of the beak, feet)
- Diphtheritic-like pseudomembranes in the oral cavity and throat
- Listlessness, reduced appetite
- Worsening of the condition when secondary infection occurs

## Impact

The cutaneous form is mostly self-limiting, but it affects racing condition and appearance; the mucosal form can cause difficulty in swallowing and breathing; secondary bacterial infection is the main aggravating factor.

## Risk factors

Mosquito season, poor loft ventilation, high stocking density and skin trauma all raise the risk of infection.

## Diagnosis and testing

- **Sample types**: pox scabs, swabs from affected tissue
- **Test method**: real-time PCR (qPCR)
- **Report turnaround**: 2-3 working days.

Signs on their own are not the same as disease. When suspicious signs appear, confirm the pathogen through laboratory testing rather than medicating on signs alone.

## Differential diagnosis

It must be distinguished from vitamin deficiency, scabbing from trauma, candidiasis (oral white plaques) and similar conditions.

## Prevention

Control mosquitoes (screens, mosquito control), reduce skin trauma, and vaccinate with pox vaccine (in endemic areas, under veterinary guidance).

## Biosecurity recommendations

Remove fallen scabs and disinfect the environment, control mosquitoes and insects, isolate sick pigeons, and keep the loft dry and well ventilated.

## Value of testing

Confirms pigeon pox and distinguishes it from oral white-plaque lesions, guiding isolation and the control of secondary infection.

## FAQ

**Can pigeon pox be transmitted to people?**

Pigeon poxvirus does not infect humans, so there is no human-pigeon zoonotic concern.

**Should the pox lesions be lanced?**

Lancing them yourself is not advisable, as it easily causes secondary infection and spread; a veterinarian should handle it.

**How long does recovery take?**

In most cutaneous cases the scabs dry and fall off and the bird recovers on its own within a few weeks, with extra nutrition and care during the recovery period.
